Some journeys are as certain as anything in the past. Magellan's last ship sailed into Sanlucar with 18 men; Amundsen left a tent at the South Pole with a letter in it, and Scott found it. Others rest on a single line in a single chronicle, a saga written down two centuries later, or a story a king told an official in Cairo. Maps of "great journeys" usually draw them all in the same confident ink.

I drew {{n_journeys}} of them, from about 600 BC to 1947: famous voyages, envoys and pilgrims, Viking crossings, lost expeditions and a handful of beautiful legends. Each route is a chain of {{n_stops}} named stops taken from the Wikipedia article about the journey, with every stop's coordinates and source recorded. Then I graded each journey by its evidence, in four inks: Proven (archaeology or several independent records), Probable (one good written source), Disputed (serious scholarly argument) and Legend (myth, legend or oral tradition). Outward legs are solid, return legs dashed, and a cross marks where a party never came back.

02 · EvidenceThe further back, the thinner the ink

Of the {{pre1450.n}} journeys that began before 1450, only {{pre1450.proven}} are proven. Of the {{post1450.n}} that began after, {{post1450.proven}} are. Before AD 500 just {{eras.0.grades.Proven|words}} of {{eras.0.n}} counts as proven: Zhang Qian's mission to Central Asia, told by a court historian within living memory and followed by documented embassies and trade. All {{grade_counts.Legend}} legends begin before 1450.

That is not because ancient travellers went less far. It is because the evidence for an ancient journey is usually one text, often written long afterwards by someone who was not there: Herodotus on the Phoenicians who sailed round Africa, the Book of the Later Han on Gan Ying, the Saga of the Greenlanders on Bjarni. Printed books, logbooks, telegrams and photographs changed that. From Columbus onwards, the question stops being "did it happen?" and becomes "exactly where?".

03 · Coming homeDid they come back?

{{return_counts.returned}} of the {{n_journeys}} parties came home whole, at least as their sources tell it. In {{return_counts.some}} more, some came back and some did not: of about 170 men with da Gama, roughly 55; of about 270 with Magellan, 18 on the Victoria; of the four men of Burke and Wills who crossed Australia, only John King. {{return_counts.never|Words}} parties never came home, and for {{return_counts.unknown|words}} nobody knows.

Coming back is usually how we know. Most of these journeys survive because somebody returned to write them down or tell them, which is why the "never returned" column is so odd. It holds the best-documented losses of all, Franklin, La Perouse and Scott, found through notes, wrecks and bodies, and two legends whose whole point is that nobody returned: Xu Fu, who sailed for the isles of the immortals in 210 BC, and the Roman legion said to have ended up in China.

07 · LimitsWhat this map can and cannot say

The grades are judgements. I set them from what each article says about its sources, using four simple rules, and I give the reason on every story card. Reasonable people would move some journeys: Marco Polo (probable here, because it rests on one book, though details are confirmed elsewhere), the Norse (proven for the crossing, which archaeology shows, not for every saga detail) or Polynesian contact with South America (disputed here, though genetics now makes some contact very likely).

Wikipedia is the source. Every route, date and debate comes from the English Wikipedia article on the journey, read in October 2026. That makes the work checkable and the sources easy to follow, but it inherits the articles' choices, gaps and errors. I checked a few facts against other sources and note them where I used them.

The lines are drawn, not logged. Stops come from named places in the articles. Between stops I drew the shortest plausible path, and I added {{n_drawn_points|int}} routing points to keep lines on sensible courses. None of these is a stop, and none is shown as one.

The selection is mine. These {{n_journeys}} are not a sample of all journeys. I chose famous ones, some lost ones and some legends, and tried to cover the world rather than only Europe. A different set would give different shares.

How I did this

Sources. The full English Wikipedia text of {{n_articles_used}} articles about these journeys and their travellers. For each journey I read the article's own account of the route, dates, evidence and debates, and noted the article and section behind every stop.

Places. {{n_stops}} stops: {{coord_sources.wikipedia}} take the coordinates of the Wikipedia article for that place, {{coord_sources.geonames}} come from the GeoNames gazetteer (a nearby town where the article names a region or an old site), and {{coord_sources.text}} are positions given in the article text itself, such as the latitude where Endurance was abandoned. Each stop is rated known ({{stop_conf.known}}), approximate ({{stop_conf.approximate}}) or conjectural ({{stop_conf.conjectural}}).

Routes. Land legs are great circles between stops. Sea legs are the shortest path on a half-degree grid of open sea (from Natural Earth coastlines, with narrow straits opened by hand), smoothed into straight runs. Distances are measured along the drawn line.

Grades. Proven: archaeology, physical remains or several independent records of the journey. Probable: one good written source, usually first-hand, with nothing serious against it. Disputed: scholars seriously argue over whether, or how far, it happened. Legend: myth, legend, oral tradition or modern speculation without evidence. "Did they come back?" follows the main source.

Speed. Only for passages with a departure and an arrival date in the article: drawn kilometres divided by days.
